# Learning-science source notes: what the evidence licenses

These notes deliberately distinguish an observed effect from a product claim. “Rejected” means an inference the cited study does **not** justify—not that the study is bad.

## 1. Desirable difficulties: performance during practice is not learning

### Schmidt & Bjork (1992), *New Conceptualizations of Practice*

- **Primary source:** Richard A. Schmidt and Robert A. Bjork, *Psychological Science*, 3(4), 207–217. [Publisher and DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1992.tb00029.x); [author-hosted paper](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Schmidt_RBjork_1992.pdf).
- **Study type and scope:** This is a theory-building synthesis by the originators, not one new randomized trial. It compares original experimental programs in motor learning and verbal memory: variable versus constant practice, blocked versus random practice, and frequent versus reduced feedback.
- **What I take:** Conditions that make acquisition look fluent can produce weaker delayed retention or transfer; conditions that cause more errors and slower gains during practice can improve later performance. Therefore an app’s “accuracy today,” streak, or modules completed is not a defensible proxy for durable learning.
- **What I reject:** “Harder is always better.” A difficulty is desirable only when it induces processing that supports the target performance and remains surmountable. The paper does not license arbitrary friction, premature MCAT passages, or withholding useful instruction.
- **Product implication:** Keep acquisition metrics and delayed/transfer metrics visibly separate. A product should be able to say “practice performance fell while evidence of learning improved.”

### Karpicke & Roediger (2008), *The Critical Importance of Retrieval for Learning*

- **Primary source:** Jeffrey D. Karpicke and Henry L. Roediger III, *Science*, 319, 966–968. [Publisher and DOI](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408).
- **Population/task:** University students learned foreign-language word pairs through study–test cycles. Once a pair was recalled correctly, the experiment independently removed it from further study and/or further testing. Delayed recall was measured one week later; learners also predicted performance.
- **Observed finding:** Repeated study after a correct retrieval added essentially no delayed-recall benefit, whereas continued retrieval produced a large benefit. Learners’ forecasts did not track their later performance.
- **What I take:** Correct once is not learned; retrieval opportunities after initial success matter. Immediate fluency is also a poor calibration signal.
- **What I reject:** This paired-associate experiment does not show that fact retrieval alone produces MCAT reasoning readiness, or that more flashcards imply DOK 2–3 performance.
- **Boundary:** Retrieval practice is table stakes. Its real value here is the warning that a progress model based on exposure or one successful response is mis-specified.

## 2. Interleaving versus blocking: train selection, not only execution

### Rohrer & Taylor (2007), *The Shuffling of Mathematics Practice Problems Boosts Learning*

- **Primary source:** Doug Rohrer and Kelli Taylor, *Instructional Science*, 35, 481–498. [University repository record and paper](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1767/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8).
- **Population/task:** College students practiced mathematics problems. Experiment 1 separated massed from spaced practice for one problem type. Experiment 2 used 25 students and compared blocked-by-type with mixed practice across several problem types; the delayed test came one week later.
- **Observed finding:** Spacing helped retention in Experiment 1. In Experiment 2, mixed practice produced much better delayed test performance even though blocked practice made the procedure easier to repeat during practice. Because each mixed item required identifying the relevant procedure, the manipulation trained strategy selection as well as execution.
- **What I take:** An MCAT tool should interleave confusable mechanisms and question types so the learner must decide *what kind of problem this is* before applying a known procedure.
- **What I reject:** A sample of 25 college students solving a constrained set of math problems does not establish a universal interleaving advantage, an optimal schedule, or an MCAT score effect. “Shuffle everything” is not the finding.

### Kornell & Bjork (2008), *Learning Concepts and Categories: Is Spacing the “Enemy of Induction”?*

- **Primary source:** Nate Kornell and Robert A. Bjork, *Psychological Science*, 19, 585–592. [PubMed record and DOI](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18578849/); [author-hosted paper](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Kornell_Bjork_2008_PsychScience.pdf).
- **Population/task:** Mostly university participants studied paintings by relatively unfamiliar artists. Works from an artist appeared blocked together or interleaved with other artists’ works; the test required attributing new paintings to artists. Participants judged which schedule helped them learn.
- **Observed finding:** Interleaving improved classification of novel exemplars, while learners tended to believe blocking had helped more. The likely instructional function is contrast: adjacent unlike categories make diagnostic differences available.
- **What I take:** Interleave categories when the target is discrimination among plausible alternatives, and ask the learner to articulate the discriminating feature.
- **What I reject:** The study does not show that temporal spacing itself caused the benefit, nor that interleaving is best during first exposure to every concept. It also does not turn new-exemplar classification into evidence of broad transfer.

### Birnbaum, Kornell, Bjork & Bjork (2013), *Why Interleaving Enhances Inductive Learning*

- **Primary source:** Monica S. Birnbaum, Nate Kornell, Elizabeth Ligon Bjork, and Robert A. Bjork, *Memory & Cognition*, 41, 392–402. [PubMed record and DOI](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23138567/).
- **Population/task:** Across three experiments, participants learned natural categories using photographs of butterflies and birds. The design manipulated interleaving and temporal spacing separately and together while controlling total spacing.
- **Observed finding:** Spacing that broke the juxtaposition of contrasting categories could hurt induction; spacing helped when it did not interrupt discrimination processing. This supports discriminative contrast, not “random mixing,” as an important mechanism.
- **What I take:** The sequencing unit should be a deliberate contrast set—e.g., two mechanisms that predict different experimental outcomes—not an undirected queue.
- **What I reject:** “Spacing and interleaving stack monotonically.” The experiments show they can interfere when spacing destroys the comparison that interleaving was meant to create.

## 3. Transfer distance: define the destination before claiming readiness

### Barnett & Ceci (2002), *When and Where Do We Apply What We Learn?*

- **Primary source:** Susan M. Barnett and Stephen J. Ceci, *Psychological Bulletin*, 128, 612–637. [PubMed record and DOI](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12081085/); [paper](https://pdf.retrievalpractice.org/transfer/Barnett_Ceci_2002.pdf).
- **Study type and scope:** A primary theoretical taxonomy and reanalysis of a century of transfer experiments, not a single intervention. It separates the learned content from dimensions of the transfer context, including knowledge domain, physical/temporal context, function, social context, and modality.
- **Observed finding:** “Far transfer” is not one outcome with one effect size. Evidence varies by *which dimension* changes; many important combinations remain untested.
- **What I take:** Readiness must be a vector of explicitly tested distances: new wording, new surface context, new passage/data representation, delayed test, mixed topic, time pressure, and ultimately a full exam-form context.
- **What I reject:** Calling any unseen question “far transfer,” or averaging heterogeneous transfer probes into one readiness percentage. New wording with the same cue and procedure may be only near transfer.

### Butler (2010), *Repeated Testing Produces Superior Transfer of Learning Relative to Repeated Studying*

- **Primary source:** Andrew C. Butler, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 36, 1118–1133. [PubMed record and DOI](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20804289/); [full paper](https://andymatuschak.org/files/papers/Butler%20-%202010%20-%20Repeated%20Testing%20Produces%20Superior%20Transfer%20of%20Learning%20Relative%20to%20Repeated.pdf).
- **Population/task:** Adult participants studied prose passages. Across four experiments they repeatedly studied or answered questions with feedback, then after one week answered identical questions, new inferential questions in the same domain, or questions applying a concept in a different domain. In Experiment 3, passages were about 500 words and transfer required short constructed responses.
- **Observed finding:** Repeated testing with feedback beat restudy not only on repeated items but also on the study’s new inference and cross-domain application questions.
- **What I take:** Retrieval can be designed around concepts and applications, not only isolated facts. A useful app should retrieve the same principle through materially different examples and representations.
- **What I reject:** “Testing causes general reasoning ability” or “any quiz produces far transfer.” The transfer items were author-designed analogical applications closely tied to concepts in short passages; they were not an independent high-stakes exam or genuinely unconstrained DOK 4 task.

## 4. Deliberate practice: the unit is a diagnosed weakness, not time spent

### Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993), *The Role of Deliberate Practice in the Acquisition of Expert Performance*

- **Primary source:** K. Anders Ericsson, Ralf Th. Krampe, and Clemens Tesch-Römer, *Psychological Review*, 100, 363–406. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.100.3.363); [paper](https://andymatuschak.org/files/papers/Ericsson%20et%20al%20-%201993%20-%20The%20role%20of%20deliberate%20practice%20in%20the%20acquisition%20of%20expert%20performance.pdf).
- **Population/task:** The most cited empirical studies compared small, selected groups of Berlin violin students rated at different skill levels (roughly ten per group), used retrospective practice estimates plus one-week diaries, and also examined professional and amateur pianists. The larger paper builds a theoretical framework across expertise domains.
- **Observed finding:** Higher-skill musicians reported more accumulated solitary practice, and deliberate practice was effortful, purposefully structured for improvement, constrained in daily duration, and distinct from mere work or play.
- **What I take:** “Practice” requires a specific improvable subskill, a task just beyond reliable execution, informative feedback, and another attempt. Minutes, questions, or pages are inputs, not progress.
- **What I reject:** A causal “10,000-hour rule,” a claim that practice explains all expertise variance, or an inference from violin practice to MCAT score gains. The grouping was not randomized; accumulated hours were substantially retrospective; selection and prior advantage remain live explanations.

### Macnamara & Maitra (2019), *The Role of Deliberate Practice in Expert Performance: Revisiting Ericsson, Krampe, & Tesch-Römer*

- **Primary source:** Brooke N. Macnamara and Megha Maitra, *Royal Society Open Science*, 6:190327. [Publisher and DOI](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.190327); [open data](https://datadryad.org/stash/dataset/doi:10.5061/dryad.68db279).
- **Population/task:** A preregistered-style direct revisit with violinists used the original study’s practice-history logic and public data, comparing “good,” “better,” and “best” performers.
- **Observed finding:** More accomplished violinists had accumulated more practice than less accomplished violinists, but the best did not report more practice than the merely better group. Practice did not cleanly separate the highest attainable levels.
- **What I take:** Deliberate practice is a design discipline, not a scalar theory of the learner. The app should improve observed error patterns without pretending time-on-task uniquely determines outcomes.
- **What I reject:** Both extremes: “practice is everything” and “practice does not matter.” The evidence supports a relationship while rejecting a simple sufficiency claim.

## 5. Cross-source design constraints

1. **Do not collapse acquisition into learning.** Show same-session accuracy separately from delayed retention.
2. **Do not collapse retention into transfer.** Label the precise novelty dimensions a probe changes.
3. **Interleave for a reason.** Use contrast sets that require strategy/cue discrimination; do not shuffle blindly.
4. **Treat a successful item as weak evidence.** Require repeated retrieval over time and across representations.
5. **Treat feedback as a loop.** Diagnose a specific error, target it, and ask for a new attempt; content consumption is not deliberate practice.
6. **Expose uncertainty.** Small samples, narrow item coverage, and untested transfer dimensions should reduce confidence, not merely lower a score.
7. **Do not equate difficulty with complexity.** Desirable difficulty is a manipulation of learning conditions; DOK is the cognitive complexity of the target performance.

## Boundary context: spacing and testing

Spacing and retrieval are well supported, but the sources above rule out the usual product shortcut. Scheduling fact recall can improve fact retention; it does not by itself establish discrimination among mechanisms, passage reasoning, transfer across representations, calibrated self-assessment, or readiness under timed exam conditions.

## 6. Metacognitive calibration: confidence is a forecast, not evidence

### Koriat (1997), *Monitoring One’s Own Knowledge During Study*

- **Primary source:** Asher Koriat, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 126, 349–370. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.126.4.349).
- **Population/task:** Four paired-associate experiments manipulated intrinsic item difficulty and extrinsic study conditions such as list repetition, within-list repetition, and presentation duration; participants made judgments of learning (JOLs) and later recalled.
- **Observed finding:** Item difficulty moved JOLs and recall in parallel, but repetition and duration improved recall more than they moved JOLs. Learners did not fully incorporate real effects of study conditions into forecasts.
- **What I take:** Confidence is an inference from available cues, not direct access to memory. Capture a prediction, then audit it against delayed performance.
- **What I reject:** Confidence is not useless, and this does not establish calibration for passage reasoning or MCAT score prediction. Paired words are a narrow DOK 1 task.

### Metcalfe & Finn (2008), *Evidence That Judgments of Learning Are Causally Related to Study Choice*

- **Primary source:** Janet Metcalfe and Bridgid Finn, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 15, 174–179. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/PBR.15.1.174); [primary paper](https://pdf.retrievalpractice.org/metacognition/5_Metcalfe_Finn_2008.pdf).
- **Population/task:** Three experiments with Columbia/Barnard students used word pairs: Experiment 1, 22 students/24 pairs; Experiment 2, 50 students/64 pairs with immediate versus delayed JOLs; Experiment 3, 24 students with forward, backward, and unrelated associates.
- **Observed finding:** Study choices followed JOLs even when those judgments were made illusory by the manipulation. Delaying JOLs reduced one bias; with asymmetric associates, learners’ restudy choices followed mistaken confidence despite much worse backward recall.
- **What I take:** Miscalibration changes behavior by telling learners what to stop studying. The app should not merely ask confidence; it should compare predicted with actual outcomes and use delayed, retrieval-grounded judgments.
- **What I reject:** The study does not say to obey every low-confidence choice or that word-pair restudy policy generalizes directly to MCAT passage selection.

### Dunlosky & Rawson (2012), *Overconfidence Produces Underachievement*

- **Primary source:** John Dunlosky and Katherine A. Rawson, *Learning and Instruction*, 22, 271–280. [Publisher and DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2011.08.003).
- **Population/task:** In two studies, college students self-paced key-term definitions, practiced cued recall, judged how much of each typed definition was correct, and stopped after judging it correct three times; final recall occurred two days later.
- **Observed finding:** More accurate self-evaluation predicted higher retention even after ruling out differential feedback, study effort, and trials to criterion. Overestimation caused premature termination of study.
- **What I take:** Learner-declared mastery is unsafe as a stopping rule. Require externally scored and delayed evidence.
- **What I reject:** Definitions are mainly DOK 1; the study validates neither “three correct” as a universal threshold nor an MCAT-readiness model.

## 7. Cognitive load: support can increase transfer when it frees capacity

### Paas & van Merriënboer (1994), *Variability of Worked Examples and Transfer of Geometrical Problem-Solving Skills*

- **Primary source:** Fred Paas and Jeroen van Merriënboer, *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 86, 122–133. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.86.1.122); [author/institution paper](https://ris.utwente.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/288323661/Paas1994variability.pdf).
- **Population/task:** Sixty Dutch secondary technical-school students (58 men, ages 19–23) were randomized, 15 per cell, to low/high variability crossed with conventional problems or worked examples. They learned CNC geometry/programming; six transfer problems differed from training and required less-obvious multi-subgoal solutions.
- **Observed finding:** Worked-example learners used about half the practice time, reported less mental effort, and transferred better. High variability improved transfer only in the worked-example condition (interaction *F*(1,56)=4.26, *p*<.05); worked examples also reduced reported transfer effort (*F*(1,56)=6.50, *p*<.025).
- **What I take:** For novices, reduce unproductive search with visible worked reasoning, then spend the freed capacity on varied cases. Track time and perceived effort alongside performance.
- **What I reject:** “Examples always beat attempts.” The sample was small and male-heavy in one vocational domain; the authors note the subjective load measure needed more validation, a physiological measure failed to distinguish conditions, and alternative mechanisms remained.
