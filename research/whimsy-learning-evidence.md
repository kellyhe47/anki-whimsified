# Concept-relevant whimsy and learning: evidence memo

Research date: 2026-08-01

## Bottom line

“Fun” is not one instructional treatment. A playful element can (1) encode a
target relationship, (2) induce positive affect without encoding the lesson, or
(3) compete with the lesson for attention. The evidence is most defensible for
the first, mixed for the second, and often negative for the third.

Concept-relevant whimsy should therefore be treated as a structured mnemonic,
not decoration. Every memorable element must map to the concept; the cue should
be faded; and delayed, neutral, cue-free tests must determine whether learning
survives.

## Evidence and boundaries

### Harp & Mayer (1998): interesting but irrelevant details can harm learning

- Primary source: Shannon F. Harp and Richard E. Mayer, *Journal of Educational
  Psychology*, 90, 414–434. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.90.3.414>
- Four experiments with 357 undergraduates found that science passages with
  interesting but irrelevant details produced lower main-idea recall and fewer
  problem-solving transfer solutions than passages without them.
- Take: extra interest can create extraneous processing or organize the lesson
  around the wrong schema.
- Reject: this does not show that all vividness, imagery, or playfulness is
  harmful. The added material was irrelevant to the learning objective.

### Bolkan, Griffin & Goodboy (2018): humorous examples can distract

- Primary source: *Communication Education*, 67, 144–164.
  <https://doi.org/10.1080/03634523.2017.1413199>
- In two randomized experiments, learners receiving humorous examples scored
  worse than learners receiving standard examples on direct tests of the
  lessons, including open-ended testing in the replication.
- Take: integrating humor into examples does not automatically make the example
  an effective mnemonic. Learners may encode the joke more strongly than the
  explanatory relationship.
- Reject: the experiments do not establish that carefully designed,
  relation-preserving mnemonic imagery must have the same effect.

### Um et al. (2012) and Plass et al. (2014): positive design can help, but not reliably

- Primary sources: Um, Plass, Hayward & Homer, *Journal of Educational
  Psychology*, 104, 485–498, <https://doi.org/10.1037/a0026609>; Plass, Heidig,
  Hayward, Homer & Um, *Learning and Instruction*, 29, 128–140,
  <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2013.02.006>.
- Um et al. randomly assigned 118 college students to positive/neutral mood and
  positive/neutral visual design conditions. Positive emotional design improved
  some learning and affective outcomes and reduced perceived task difficulty.
- Plass et al. partly replicated the pattern: appealing design improved
  comprehension, but transfer effects depended on the specific combination of
  color and shape and were absent in one study.
- Take: pleasant design can increase motivation or generative engagement and can
  make a task feel less difficult. It does not necessarily add harmful load.
- Reject: feeling better or reporting lower difficulty is not proof of durable
  learning, and these visual-design findings do not prove that humor or whimsy
  improves MCAT transfer.

### McDaniel & Einstein (1986): bizarreness works only under constraints

- Primary source: *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and
  Cognition*, 12, 54–65. <https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.12.1.54>
- Across five experiments, bizarre imagery improved recall in mixed-list
  conditions but not consistently when all material was bizarre. The advantage
  appeared with imagery processing rather than semantic processing. The authors
  identified distinctiveness as a likely mechanism.
- Take: an unusual image can become a discriminable retrieval cue.
- Reject: “make everything weird.” If every item is equally bizarre, the
  distinctiveness advantage can disappear; bizarreness alone is not the useful
  association.

### Dretzke & Levin (1996): mnemonic success does not guarantee transfer

- Primary source: *Contemporary Educational Psychology*, 21, 83–93.
  <https://doi.org/10.1006/ceps.1996.0007>
- High-school students taught a mnemonic outperformed controls on recall and
  application for the instructed material, but did not spontaneously transfer
  the mnemonic strategy to new material on the following day.
- Take: a mapped mnemonic can support access to the material it encodes.
- Reject: learners will independently invent or transfer the strategy, or that
  better recall establishes higher-order readiness.

### Isen & Daubman (1984); Isen, Daubman & Nowicki (1987): positive affect can broaden processing

- Primary sources: *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 47,
  1206–1217, <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.47.6.1206>; and 52, 1122–1131,
  <https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.52.6.1122>.
- Positive-affect inductions led participants to use broader categories and
  improved performance on two creative problem-solving tasks.
- Take: positive affect can alter cognitive organization and flexibility. This
  makes the “more associations” mechanism plausible.
- Reject: these were not instructional-memory or MCAT studies. Broader
  association can also be harmful when a task requires precise discrimination.

## Product interpretation

Whimsy can increase mental load because it introduces another representation to
process. That load is useful only when processing the whimsical representation
also processes the target relationship. A mitochondrion wearing a funny hat is
decoration; a character whose actions accurately enact proton pumping, the
gradient, and ATP synthase is a candidate encoding structure.

The design rules are:

1. Map every whimsical element to a fact, relation, direction, or causal step.
2. Remove elements that can be remembered without reconstructing the concept.
3. Use one distinctive cue selectively; do not make every screen maximally odd.
4. Let learners personalize or generate the association, but verify scientific
   accuracy.
5. Fade the cue during retrieval practice and remove it during formal testing.
6. Measure perceived effort, response time, delayed neutral recall, and novel
   neutral transfer separately.
7. Compare three equal-time conditions: plain instruction, decorative whimsy,
   and concept-relevant whimsy.

The evidence currently licenses a DOK 1 memory hypothesis. It does not yet
license the claim that whimsy improves DOK 2–3 MCAT performance. That claim must
be earned by neutral, delayed, unfamiliar passage testing.
