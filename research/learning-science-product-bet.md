# Learning science for a readiness product

Research date: 2026-07-30

## Thesis

Spacing and retrieval practice are necessary infrastructure, not a product thesis. The stronger opportunity is to measure and train **cue-free transfer plus metacognitive calibration**.

The current tools mostly ask, “Can you answer another item that already announces its subject, format, and likely procedure?” Readiness asks a harder question: **Can you notice which knowledge applies in a new, mixed, timed context—and know when your answer is not trustworthy?**

That distinction changes the product from a content scheduler into an evidence engine.

## Primary-source findings: what I take and what I reject

### 1. Richard Schmidt and Robert Bjork (1992): performance during practice is not learning

Primary source: Richard A. Schmidt & Robert A. Bjork, “New Conceptualizations of Practice: Common Principles in Three Paradigms Suggest New Concepts for Training,” *Psychological Science*, 3(4), 207–217. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1992.tb00029.x); [author-hosted paper](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Schmidt_RBjork_1992.pdf).

**What I take**

Across verbal and motor paradigms, conditions that make acquisition look smooth can damage delayed retention and transfer; conditions that depress immediate performance can improve them. The product implication is severe: in-session accuracy, speed of module completion, and a rising streak can be negatively related to the evidence we actually want.

**What I reject**

I reject “difficulty is good.” A difficulty is desirable only when it induces processing that supports the target retention or transfer and remains solvable. Random friction, cryptic interfaces, premature problem solving, or overwhelming passages can reduce both practice performance and learning. The paper supports a dissociation between acquisition and later performance; it does not license maximizing struggle.

### 2. Nate Kornell and Robert Bjork (2008): interleaving can improve induction while feeling worse

Primary source: Nate Kornell & Robert A. Bjork, “Learning Concepts and Categories: Is Spacing the ‘Enemy of Induction?’” *Psychological Science*, 19(6), 585–592. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18578849/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02127.x).

Participants studied paintings by unfamiliar artists either blocked by artist or interleaved across artists, then classified new paintings.

**What I take**

Interleaving improved classification of new exemplars, while learners judged blocking as more effective. This joins discrimination and calibration: mixed practice can force learners to compare categories, but subjective fluency tells them the opposite. An app should therefore expose contrasts between confusable strategies and show the learner the gap between felt learning and demonstrated transfer.

**What I reject**

I reject “always interleave everything.” The task involved visual-category induction with repeated exemplars and immediate category labels. It does not show that novices should encounter unscaffolded, fully mixed MCAT passages before they possess usable schemas. It also confounds interleaving with temporal spacing. The product needs a progression from initial blocked schema formation to interleaved discrimination, not a universal shuffle button.

### 3. Doug Rohrer and Kelli Taylor (2007): mixed mathematics practice trains problem selection

Primary source: Doug Rohrer & Kelli Taylor, “The Shuffling of Mathematics Practice Problems Boosts Learning,” *Instructional Science*, 35, 481–498. [University repository](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1767/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8).

One experiment separated spacing from massing; another compared blocked and mixed practice of multiple mathematics problem types, with a delayed test one week later.

**What I take**

Mixed practice improved delayed performance because the learner had to choose a procedure from the problem, not merely execute the procedure named by the worksheet section. That maps directly to MCAT readiness: a topic-filtered Qbank often gives away the first strategic decision—what kind of problem this is.

**What I reject**

I reject treating the result as evidence for far transfer or DOK 4. The delayed problems still belonged to the practiced families. Interleaving trained discrimination among known types; it did not establish transfer across domains or synthesis across sources. A mixed Qbank is a stronger DOK 2 instrument, not automatically a readiness instrument.

### 4. Mary Gick and Keith Holyoak (1980): possessing an analogy is not spontaneously using it

Primary source: Mary L. Gick & Keith J. Holyoak, “Analogical Problem Solving,” *Cognitive Psychology*, 12(3), 306–355. [University of Michigan repository](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/c0edbf58-93fd-41d4-91d6-22ae89d1adca); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(80)90013-4).

Participants encountered a military “convergence” solution and later faced Duncker’s radiation problem, whose solution shared the same relational structure but different surface features. Transfer rose sharply when participants were explicitly told to use the earlier story; without the hint, spontaneous transfer fell markedly.

**What I take**

This is the product bet. Transfer failure is often a **retrieval-and-noticing failure**, not absence of stored knowledge. A tool that labels the topic, filters the set, presents a lesson immediately before the problem, or announces “use enzyme kinetics” has supplied the crucial cue and therefore cannot claim to have measured readiness.

**What I reject**

I reject turning one classic laboratory analogy into a universal transfer coefficient. The source and target were stylized insight problems, and explicit hints can alter task demands. The study demonstrates a measurement threat—cues can manufacture apparent transfer—not a complete model of MCAT performance. The app must validate the pattern with its own delayed, cue-free, cross-context item pairs.

### 5. John Sweller (1988): novice problem solving can consume the capacity needed to learn

Primary source: John Sweller, “Cognitive Load During Problem Solving: Effects on Learning,” *Cognitive Science*, 12(2), 257–285. [Publisher](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1207/s15516709cog1202_4); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1202_4).

Sweller argued, with experimental evidence and a computational account, that means–ends problem solving can impose processing demands that leave too little capacity for schema acquisition.

**What I take**

Assessment difficulty and instructional difficulty must be separated. Early learning should use worked examples, completion problems, and fading so the student can build schemas. Cue-free transfer belongs later, after prerequisites are demonstrated. The same hard passage can be a useful assessment and a poor first lesson.

**What I reject**

I reject “reduce cognitive load” as a global goal. Test-day performance requires managing intrinsic complexity, and germane comparison or retrieval can feel effortful. A frictionless lesson may produce fluency without durable learning. The product should remove load unrelated to the target reasoning while preserving the complexity that the target performance actually requires.

### 6. K. Anders Ericsson, Ralf Krampe, and Clemens Tesch-Römer (1993): practice becomes deliberate through feedback and correction, not duration

Primary source: K. Anders Ericsson, Ralf T. Krampe, & Clemens Tesch-Römer, “The Role of Deliberate Practice in the Acquisition of Expert Performance,” *Psychological Review*, 100(3), 363–406. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.100.3.363); [paper](https://andymatuschak.org/files/papers/Ericsson%20et%20al%20-%201993%20-%20The%20role%20of%20deliberate%20practice%20in%20the%20acquisition%20of%20expert%20performance.pdf).

**What I take**

Practice should target a specific weakness just beyond current reliable performance, demand full attention, provide interpretable feedback, and create another opportunity to execute the corrected process. “Questions completed” and “minutes studied” lack the defining loop.

**What I reject**

I reject the popular reduction to a universal hours rule and reject applying elite violinists’ retrospective practice estimates directly to short-term MCAT preparation. The paper’s expert-performance evidence is domain-specific and partly correlational. It supports the structure of a feedback loop more strongly than it supports any fixed dose or guarantee of expertise.

### 7. John Dunlosky and Katherine Rawson (2012): overconfidence changes study control and damages retention

Primary source: John Dunlosky & Katherine A. Rawson, “Overconfidence Produces Underachievement: Inaccurate Self Evaluations Undermine Students’ Learning and Retention,” *Learning and Instruction*, 22(4), 271–280. [Publisher](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959475211000685); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2011.08.003).

College students studied key-term definitions, attempted cued recall, judged whether their answers were correct, and used those judgments to regulate study. Premature judgments of mastery led to stopping practice too soon and worse later retention.

**What I take**

Calibration is causal infrastructure for self-regulated learning, not a decorative confidence score. The app must capture confidence **before feedback**, score calibration against outcomes, and change the next assignment when a learner is confidently wrong. A correct answer with low confidence and a correct answer with calibrated high confidence are different learning states.

**What I reject**

I reject equating confidence with competence or punishing confidence itself. Some accurate learners should be highly confident. The target is conditional accuracy—whether confidence tracks correctness—measured across enough observations. These definition-recall experiments also do not prove that the same calibration function transfers unchanged to passage-level reasoning.

### 8. Jeffrey Karpicke, Andrew Butler, and Henry Roediger (2009): learners do not naturally choose the strategies that produce durable learning

Primary source: Jeffrey D. Karpicke, Andrew C. Butler, & Henry L. Roediger III, “Metacognitive Strategies in Student Learning: Do Students Practise Retrieval When They Study on Their Own?” *Memory*, 17(4), 471–479. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19358016/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658210802647009); [author-hosted paper](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2009/2009_Karpicke_Butler_Roediger.pdf).

The authors surveyed 177 college students about self-directed study strategies. Retrieval practice was not a dominant spontaneously chosen strategy, and many students used testing mainly to diagnose rather than to learn.

**What I take**

The app cannot merely offer effective modes and expect rational selection. It must schedule the learning actions, explain why an effortful result can be more diagnostic, and prevent the student from converting every session into comfortable rereading or topic-filtered repetition.

**What I reject**

I reject using a self-report survey as direct evidence that a particular adaptive scheduler improves achievement. The study establishes a metacognitive-strategy gap, not the efficacy of a specific intervention. Product behavior still needs experimental validation.

## Requested practitioner article: UNC Learning Center

Requested source: The Learning Center at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, [“Memorization Strategies”](https://learningcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/enhancing-your-memory/).

This is a university learning-support handout, not a primary experiment. I include it as evidence of the advice students actually encounter, but I do not use it to establish a causal learning-science claim. Its “Works consulted” section points mainly to popular books rather than the original experiments.

**What I take**

- It correctly warns that familiarity from rereading can masquerade as memory and recommends active self-testing.
- It recommends delaying self-tests by hours or days, which is directionally aligned with measuring retention rather than immediate fluency.
- It recommends distributed practice and describes gradually increasing intervals.
- It tells students to connect new information to existing knowledge, create meaningful groupings, and use retrieval-friendly cues.
- It acknowledges that effortful techniques may initially feel strange and encourages strategy experimentation rather than prescribing every tactic to every learner.

**What I reject**

- The article says memory tricks can lead to understanding and higher-order thinking. That is too broad. A mnemonic can improve access to a fact while leaving relational understanding and transfer untouched. Memory is a prerequisite for much higher-order thinking, not evidence that higher-order thinking occurred.
- Its interleaving example alternates science vocabulary, historical dates, and mathematics problems. The strongest interleaving evidence above concerns discriminating among **confusable categories or problem types**. Switching unrelated subjects may create spacing or attention benefits, but it does not necessarily train method selection within a domain.
- The article says spacing can make learners “more certain of mastery.” Spacing may improve retention, but certainty is a metacognitive judgment that can still be wrong. The app should measure confidence–accuracy calibration rather than treating spaced exposure as warranted certainty.
- “Writing appears to help,” multisensory advice, sleep, exercise, and memory-palace guidance may be useful in bounded contexts, but this page does not provide primary evidence that each tactic improves MCAT transfer. They should be optional strategies, not readiness signals.
- The claim that visual and spatial techniques “free up” working memory is underspecified. A representation reduces load only when it organizes the relevant relations for that learner; an elaborate mnemonic can also add extraneous load.

**Product implication**

The handout is a good memory-tool menu. It is not a readiness model. The app should preserve that distinction: offer encoding aids when memory is the bottleneck, then separately test discrimination, cue-free transfer, timing, and calibration.

## What the evidence says together

The useful synthesis is not “make studying harder.”

1. **Build the schema without wasteful load.** Use concise instruction, worked examples, and completion problems for novices.
2. **Fade support into retrieval.** Require the learner to produce the next step rather than recognize it.
3. **Interleave confusable families.** Remove topic headers so the learner must select a strategy.
4. **Increase transfer distance deliberately.** Change surface features, representation, and context while preserving the underlying relation.
5. **Remove the hint.** A transfer item does not count as readiness evidence if the interface has already named the relevant concept.
6. **Collect confidence before feedback.** Measure confidence–accuracy calibration and react especially to confident errors.
7. **Use delayed evidence.** Immediate success can be acquisition fluency; readiness requires later, first-attempt performance.

## The bet

### Bet: spontaneous structural transfer, calibrated by the learner, is the missing readiness construct

I would bet the app on the Gick–Holyoak finding: people can possess a usable solution and still fail to recognize that it applies when surface cues change.

Every reviewed tool can count exposure, completion, correctness, and time. Blueprint can classify reasoning skills; Khan can explain a procedure; Jack Westin can simulate the interface; Math Academy can estimate topic mastery. None of the observed tools built a visible measurement loop around this question:

> Without being told the topic, can the student recognize the deep structure of a new problem, select the relevant knowledge, execute it under time, and accurately judge whether the result is trustworthy?

### What that forces the app to build

Build a **transfer-and-calibration ladder**, not another Qbank:

1. **Source model:** teach one underlying principle with a worked example and a rationale.
2. **Near transfer:** change values and surface wording while preserving representation.
3. **Discrimination:** interleave a confusable problem that requires a different principle; ask the learner to choose the method before solving.
4. **Representation transfer:** express the same structure as prose, graph, table, experiment, or unfamiliar notation.
5. **Farther transfer:** place the principle in a different scientific context and remove topic labels and just-in-time lesson cues.
6. **Cue-free mixed probe:** deliver the item later, timed, among unrelated items, with no indication that it connects to prior work.
7. **Confidence and abstention:** require a probability judgment or an “insufficient basis” choice before feedback.
8. **Contrastive feedback:** explain not only why the answer is right, but which surface cue was misleading, which structural cue should have triggered retrieval, and why the nearest competing method fails.
9. **Re-probe:** generate or author a structurally isomorphic but genuinely new item after a delay. Parameter swaps do not count.

### The readiness number

Do not report “73% ready.” Report evidence with boundaries:

- memory: delayed retrieval of component knowledge;
- discrimination: selection among confusable methods;
- transfer: first-attempt performance by measured transfer distance;
- timing: accuracy conditional on test-relevant pacing;
- calibration: confidence–accuracy alignment and confident-error rate;
- evidence quality: number of novel, delayed, cue-free observations and the contexts sampled.

When evidence is sparse, the product should say **“not enough evidence.”** When memory is strong but transfer is weak, it should say exactly that. Readiness should rise only from novel, delayed, mixed, timed, cue-free performance—not from content consumed.

That is what none of the reviewed products visibly does, and it is the difference between reassuring a student and measuring whether they can carry knowledge into the room where it matters.
